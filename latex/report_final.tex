\section{Introduction}
 
\subsection{Overview}
This report investigates whether sex exerts predictive power on medical insurance premiums after controlling for baseline demographics, health metrics, and financial histories—specifically age, body mass index (BMI), risk score, and total claims paid. Motivation comes from a collective interest in whether sex-based discrimination is present in the context of medical insurance applicants. Such an influence could suggest that insurance institutions operate under systemically discriminatory practices as it pertains to sex.
 
\subsection{The Data}
 
The dataset used for this analysis was sourced from Kaggle and compiled by Mohan Krishna Thalla. According to the creator's methodology, the data was aggregated, cleaned, and prepared using public health information gathered from multiple verified sources, including the Centers for Medicare \& Medicaid Services (CMS), the U.S. Department of Health \& Human Services (HHS), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's (CDC) National Health Interview Survey (NHIS). The dataset provides information on approximately 100,000 individuals, including their demographics, socioeconomic status, health conditions, lifestyle factors, and medical expenditures. As mentioned, age and BMI will serve as a primary basis for analysis.
 
Due to severe right-skew in the response variable, we applied a mathematically shifted log transformation: $\log(\text{annual\_premium} - \min(\text{annual\_premium}) + 1)$. This handles values close to zero, normalizes the extreme right skew of the insurance costs, and is necessary to better satisfy the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions required for linear regression.
 
The data we are using can be found \href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/mohankrishnathalla/medical-insurance-cost-prediction/data}{here}, which comes from the data aggregation website \href{https://www.kaggle.com}{Kaggle}.

\subsection{The Case for Regression}
 
There are many factors to consider when modeling medical insurance premiums, especially in the context of possible systemic discrimination. We turn to linear regression to formally assess whether sex is a significant predictor of premium once other covariates are controlled.
 
We examined two primary models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\log Y=\beta_0+\beta_1\cdot\text{Sex}$
    \item $\log Y=\beta_0+\beta_1\cdot\text{Sex}+\beta_2\cdot\text{Age}+\beta_3\cdot\text{BMI}+\beta_4\cdot \text{Risk Score}+\beta_5\cdot\log\text{Claims Paid}$
\end{enumerate}
 
Model 1 serves as a baseline showing the raw sex--premium relationship without covariate adjustment; Model 2 is our adjusted specification. To formally select between candidate specifications, we performed subset selection using Mallow's $C_p$. We began with a full model that added a sex--risk-score interaction term to Model 2, since such an interaction would directly operationalize the hypothesis that risk score affects premiums differently by sex---the most direct formal test of the discrimination question. We then fit all nested candidate models obtained by removing covariates one at a time.
 
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images/mallows.png}
  \caption{Mallow's $C_p$ plotted against number of covariates $p$. The diagonal reference line corresponds to the ideal $C_p \approx p + 1$.}
  \label{fig:mallows}
\end{figure}
 
The $C_p$ criterion selected the 5-covariate model (Model 2 above), indicating that adding the sex--risk-score interaction did not sufficiently improve fit to justify the additional parameter. We verified this result directly: in the full model with the interaction included, the coefficient for sex$\,\times\,$risk-score had estimate $\hat{\beta} = \textit{$0.0282847$}$ with standard error $\textit{$0.0201192$}$ and $p = \textit{ $0.160$}$, providing no evidence that the effect of risk score on premium differs by sex. Both the $C_p$ criterion and the direct hypothesis test on the interaction term point to the same conclusion, so we proceed with Model 2, in which sex enters the model only as an intercept shift.

We now interpret these results. Testing the sex coefficient with $H_0:\beta_1=0$ versus $H_1:\beta_1\neq 0$, we find $p\text{-values}$ of approximately $0.14$ (Model 1) and $0.13$ (Model 2, the full model), both of which are \textbf{above} the $0.05$ significance threshold. We therefore \textbf{fail to reject} the null hypothesis that sex has no effect on log premium in either specification. This is consistent with the visual intuition from our bivariate plots, where the male and female fitted lines were essentially indistinguishable for every covariate examined.
 
The practical magnitude of the estimated sex effect reinforces this conclusion. In the full model, the coefficient on Sex (Male) is $0.0075$, meaning that---holding age, BMI, risk score, and claims history constant---the average difference in log premium between males and females is less than $1\%$. Even if this were statistically significant, it would be of negligible practical importance.
 
The $R^2$ values further contextualize the story. Model 1, with sex as the only predictor, explains a negligible $0.002\%$ of the variance in log premiums. The full model explains approximately $17.9\%$, confirming that most explanatory power comes from the non-sex covariates rather than sex itself.
 
The other coefficients in the full model are all statistically significant at the $p<0.001$ level. Age has the largest standardized effect ($\hat{\beta}_2 = 1.485$), followed by BMI ($\hat{\beta}_3 = 0.043$), log claims paid ($\hat{\beta}_5 = 0.0026$), and risk score, which has a small negative coefficient ($\hat{\beta}_4 = -0.0083$). The negative sign on risk score is initially counterintuitive---one might expect higher-risk individuals to pay higher premiums---but in a model that already conditions on age, BMI, and claims history, the residual variation in risk score captures something different from what risk score captures in isolation, and can plausibly take a small negative sign. This is worth flagging as an interesting feature of the fit.

\section{Conclusion}
 
The motivating question for this report was whether sex is a significant predictor of medical insurance premiums after controlling for demographic and health covariates. Across all of our analyses---the raw bivariate comparison, the adjusted regression, the sex--risk-score interaction test, and the formal $C_p$ model-selection procedure---the evidence consistently points in the same direction: we find no statistical or practical support for a sex-based difference in premiums in this dataset. The coefficient on sex is small and not significant, the interaction with risk score is not significant, and visual inspection shows male and female patterns that are essentially indistinguishable across every covariate we examined.
 
That said, our analysis has meaningful limitations. The full model explains only about $18\%$ of the variance in log premiums, leaving the majority of variation unexplained. There may be unobserved confounders---smoking status, geographic region, prior medical conditions, plan type, or socioeconomic factors---that, if included, could change the picture. A negative finding in a limited specification is not the same as evidence of equal treatment in the underlying insurance system. Future work should incorporate additional covariates available in the broader dataset (the original Kaggle file includes smoking, region, chronic conditions, and plan type) to further isolate the sex--premium relationship. An additional caveat is that the dataset has been curated and cleaned from multiple upstream sources, which may have smoothed real-world variation; results should be understood in that context.
 
What we \emph{can} confidently state is that risk score and log total claims paid are significantly associated with insurance premiums, consistent with the intuitive expectation that insurers price premiums in part on assessed health risk and historical claims behavior.
